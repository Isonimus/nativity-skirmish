# What this is

> *Every year they come out of the box, stand in the same places, and do nothing at all
> until January.*

A skirmish game for two players, played with a nativity display — a *belén* — using the
figures you already own. There is nothing to buy, nothing to assemble and nothing to
paint. The set your family has kept in a box since before you could read is the terrain,
the models and the objectives.

This manual assumes you have never played a wargame. It also assumes you own a belén whose
contents nobody wrote down, which is why the rules never ask who a figure depicts. They
ask what it visibly is: how tall, how many legs, what it is holding. Two households with
completely different sets can play each other and get the same game.

## 1. What you need

- A nativity display, or any collection of figures standing on a table.
- Two D6.
- A strip of card and something to cut it with.
- Removable putty, or anything else that marks a base without marking it permanently.

That is the whole list. There is no measuring tape, because The Conversion Table §1 has you
cut one.

## 2. How a game goes

Two households are on the road. The Star moves across the display, one feature at a time,
and the light it casts is worth points to whoever stands under it. Models walk, block one
another, take things out of each other's hands, and occasionally get frightened. After six
rounds you count up.

Nothing is destroyed to win. **Removing a model scores nothing**, in any scenario in this
book. A player who has lost every figure they own can still win on points, and a player
who has swept the board can lose. This is the single decision the rest of the rules are
built around, and it is why the game can be played with a set that belonged to somebody's
grandmother.

## 3. How to read it

Read The Conversion Table with the set in front of you and work through it once — about
ten minutes, and you never do it again. Read The Core Loop before the first game. Read
Scenario 01 when you sit down to play. The Play Sheet is the one page worth printing.

Section numbers are addresses. They do not change, and new sections are added at the end
rather than inserted, so a reference written today still points at the same rule in ten
years.

## 4. On the tone

The rules are written in the register of a serious wargame manual because that is funnier
than winking, and because a game explained seriously is a game you can actually play. The
jokes are in the subject matter. The procedures are not jokes and are meant to work at a
table.

Nothing here is devotional and nothing here is a parody of anyone's belief. It is a set of
figures, standing on a table, being taken seriously as a set of figures standing on a
table.
